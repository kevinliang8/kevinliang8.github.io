Current projects

Learnability of saltationUnder review at Laboratory Phonology

Collaborator: Gemma Repiso Puigdelliura

Catalan phonology exhibits a well-known pattern of final devoicing, but a subset of stems displays an unusual alternation known as saltation, in which a voiced fricative alternates with a voiceless affricate rather than the expected voiceless fricative (e.g., [bɔʒə] ~ [bɔt͡ʃ] and not *[bɔʃ]). Saltatory alternations have been of considerable theoretical interest because they are underivable in classical Optimality Theory and have been argued to be difficult or unstable for learners.

Previous work has shown that both adults and infants with short-term laboratory exposure tend to repair saltation by overgeneralizing simpler phonological processes. It has remained unknown whether children learning a language that actually contains saltation can acquire and generalize the pattern productively.

In Fall 2025, I spent three months in Catalonia as a visiting graduate student at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona. Using the Central Fixation Procedure, I tested whether 24-month-old Catalan-learning children generalize saltation to novel words or repair it with final devoicing. Children related novel feminine forms to masculine forms with saltation applied, but not to forms with final devoicing applied. In other words, they extend saltation to new words, even though Catalan-speaking adults extend it only about half the time. We argue that this reflects lexical dilution: saltating stems are few, frequent, and learned early, so they make up a larger share of a young child’s lexicon than an adult’s.

  • Under review at Laboratory Phonology (manuscript)
  • Presented at LabPhon 20, Montréal, June 2026 (slides)
  • Covered in Línia Vallès and Parets al dia
Catalan wug testIn revision at Phonology

Collaborators: Victoria Mateu and Bruce Hayes

Catalan phonology includes numerous consonant alternations found at the right edge of stems, such as [san-ə] ~ [sa] ‘healthy-fem./masc.’ These alternations have been subjected to extensive analysis and have been frequently used as the basis of problem sets and computational studies. They bear on several important research questions, including exceptionality, opacity, and saltation. However, no study had investigated the extent to which speakers actually generalize the patterns attested in the lexicon. Our study addresses this gap with two “wug test” experiments, one using elicited production, the other using acceptability judgments. Speakers frequency-match exceptionful patterns in the lexicon. They also extend opaque and saltatory patterns productively, but less often than the lexicon would predict.

  • In revision at Phonology (submitted version)
  • Presented at the Annual Meeting on Phonology, Rutgers University, November 2024
  • Invited talks at California State University, Fresno (April 2025) and the Centre de Lingüística Teòrica, Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona (November 2025)
Phonotactics and morpheme discoveryFirst experiment published in Journal of Child Language (2025)

Collaborator: Megha Sundara

By 6 months, English-learning infants are able to relate novel words suffixed with -s, like babs and teeps, embedded in passages to the stems bab and teep, demonstrating an early sensitivity to morphological relatedness. At the same time, infants are also becoming sensitive to phonotactics. We found that 6-month-old English-learning infants relate nonce forms suffixed with the [-z] allomorph but not the [-s] allomorph of the English -s suffix. Additionally, 6-month-olds only related nonce forms suffixed with [-z] when the resulting stem would be phonotactically legal. These results show that morpheme decomposition is not obligatory at 6 months. Instead, infants’ ability to decompose potentially suffixed words is constrained by their developing knowledge of phonotactic restrictions in English.

  • Experiment 1 published in Journal of Child Language (Liang & Sundara 2025); full manuscript in preparation
  • My MA thesis is based on an earlier version of this work and includes other experiments from the project
  • Presented at LabPhon 19 (Seoul, June 2024), ICIS (Glasgow, July 2024), and BUCLD 49 (Boston, November 2024; Paula Menyuk Award)
  • Invited talk at Chosun University, Gwangju, August 2026
Encoding phonetic variation in infancyPresented at ICIS 2026; manuscript in preparation

Collaborators: Ekaterina (Katya) Khlystova and Megha Sundara

Infants are exposed to substantial phonetic variability in speech and must determine which variants should be encoded in their lexical representations. Word-final /t/ in American English provides a particularly informative case, as it is frequently realized as a glottal stop [ʔ] in infant-directed speech. Across two Headturn Preference Procedure experiments with 14-month-old English-learning infants, we tested whether infants encode the canonical [t], the frequent [ʔ] variant, or an underspecified coda. Infants recognized familiar /t/-final words produced with both [ʔ] and canonical [t], but not minimal coda mispronunciations. These results show that early lexical representations tolerate phonetic variability while retaining sufficient phonological detail to support word recognition.

  • Presented at ICIS 2026, Panama City, July 2026
  • Manuscript in preparation

Syntactic category acquisitionPublished in the BUCLD 46 Proceedings (2022)

Collaborator: Charles Yang

An important task faced by children during language acquisition is the formation of syntactic categories from individual words. Distributional cues such as frequent frames have been proposed, but their utility under realistic learning situations remains unclear. Since frequent frames are lexical, they result in a proliferation of word classes that do not correspond to recognized syntactic categories. We propose the Category Frame Learner (CFL), a model which uses the Tolerance Principle to combine lexical frames into category frames defined over syntactic categories.

  • Published in the BUCLD 46 Proceedings, with Diana Marsala and Charles Yang
  • Poster at BUCLD 46, November 2021 (video, poster); talk at the LSA Annual Meeting, January 2022
Glottalic stops in Q’eqchi’Poster at the Acoustical Society of America (2019)

Advisor: Jianjing Kuang

In Mayan languages, glottalic stops are typically ejectives, but bilabial and uvular stops can vary between more ejective-like and more implosive-like realizations. Using recordings from the UCLA Phonetics Lab Archive, we examined whether the realization of glottalic stops in Q’eqchi’ varies systematically by phonetic context or by speaker.

  • Poster at the Acoustical Society of America, San Diego, December 2019 (abstract, poster)
Rising changed tones in TaishaneseAbstract accepted at the Penn Linguistics Conference (2020)

Advisor: Eugene Buckley

Taishanese is a Sinitic language of southern China with a “changed tone” process in which a level or falling tone is realized as a rising tone, which does not otherwise occur in the language. Using field recordings of Taishanese speakers, I developed an Optimality Theory analysis of the pattern that proposes a new constraint restricting consecutive level tones.

  • Accepted as a talk at the 44th Penn Linguistics Conference, March 2020, which was cancelled due to COVID-19 (abstract)
